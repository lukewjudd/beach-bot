Beach calls

About the data

How often

The bot runs every hour from 4:15am to 9:15pm, Sydney time. The page picks up new scores on its own. The 6:15am run lands just after Beachwatch's daily update.

What a score means

Every day is cut into three sessions, the same every day of the week: morning (sunrise to 11am), midday (11am to 2pm) and evening (2pm to sunset). Every spot is scored out of 10 for each, on its best two hours. Warmth counts where the day's heading: a cool 7am on a day going to 30° is a warm-day swim, but not after a southerly change. 8/10 or better is worth it and goes in the shared calendar, which posts up to two days ahead. Further out is an early forecast: a guide, not a plan. 0 means ruled out (pollution, storms, too rough, closed).

How swell and wind are worked out

The forecast describes the sea well offshore: a main swell, sometimes a second one, and chop from the local wind, each with a height, a direction and a period (the seconds between waves). What matters for a swim is what's left of that by the time it reaches the sand, and that's different at every beach. Here's how we get from one to the other.

1. Find the open ocean

From just off each beach we draw a line every 2°, out to 30 km, over the OpenStreetMap coastline. Lines that make it all the way are open ocean: swell from those directions has a clear run in. Lines that hit land are blocked, usually by the beach's own headlands.

2. Let it bend

Swell isn't a beam of light. As it reaches shallower water it bends toward the shore, and some spreads sideways into the shadow behind a headland, so the edge of the window is a fade, not a cut. Long-period groundswell feels the bottom sooner and bends further: it wraps into corners a short wind swell can't reach. Swell that arrives side-on also spreads along more of the coast, which makes it smaller.

3. Work out how much gets in

Put those together and every spot gets a share for each direction and period. The geometry is then checked against what locals already know about each beach (from surf guides, Beachsafe and councils): the two are blended, so the model can't stray far from what's known to happen. Harbour beaches have no open ocean to measure, so they go on local knowledge alone.

4. Size it up

Longer swell carries more energy for its height, so it breaks bigger. At a swimming beach the effect is gentle; at a surf break it's stronger, because a short, weak swell barely registers on a reef or bank.

Each wave train is worked out on its own direction, then they're added up by energy. So a big south groundswell can miss a beach that faces north-east while the east wind chop still gets in. The result is the height at the beach, which is what the calm-water and surf scores are based on.

5. Then the wind

Last comes the wind, and what matters most is which way it blows across the beach. Offshore wind comes off the land and out over the water: it flattens the surface, holds the waves up so they break cleanly, and keeps the water clear. Onshore wind comes in off the sea and piles chop on top of whatever swell is running, so even a small day turns messy and murky. Cross-shore sits in between.

Which direction counts as offshore depends on which way each beach faces, so it's worked out spot by spot. Strength matters too: under about 8 km/h the water stays glassy whichever way it blows, an offshore stops helping past about 25 km/h, and strong gusts count against any wind.

6. Is the water clean?

Rain washes stormwater into the sea, and some beaches take it worse than others. If NSW Beachwatch has a forecast for the day, that's what counts: "pollution likely" rules a beach out, "possible" costs points. If not, the bot goes on the rain that's actually fallen. Each beach has its own limits, from its Beachwatch grades: open, well-flushed beaches shrug off a shower, enclosed ones don't.

Rain also clouds the water for snorkelling for a couple of days, so the snorkelling spots lose their "good snorkelling" bonus until it clears.

7. How warm will it feel?

A swim feels different at 19° and 29°. But a session's coldest hour isn't the whole story: a 7am dip on a day heading to 30° is a warm-day swim. So mornings count the warmth still to come (to early afternoon) and evenings keep the afternoon's, both 3° under, so only properly warm days get the full bonus. After a southerly change, an evening counts the temperature it actually is.

8. The sky

Storms rule a session out: lightning and water don't mix. Rain and heavy cloud cost points, warm air adds them. Water temperature only ever helps: warm water earns a few points, cool water is just noted (Sydney's water is cool much of the year, and that's no reason not to go). After a few days of north-easterlies the bot also warns that cold upwelling is likely.

9. Ocean pools and the tide

Pools stay flat when the beaches are rough, so they're scored differently: the walls keep the chop out, and what matters is big swell washing over them, which happens around high tide. Some pools are best at a particular tide (Wylie's and McIver's at low, Giles at mid), some are drained for cleaning one day a week, and the gated ones keep opening hours.

10. Bluebottles

From November to March, a day of north-easterlies or southerlies at 20 km/h or more can blow bluebottles onto the eastern beaches. Maroubra, open to both, cops it most; harbour beaches are spared.

Where the numbers come from

  • Open-Meteo: wind, air temperature, rain, storms and UV, plus swell, sea temperature and tides from its marine forecast.
  • Bureau of Meteorology: rain that's actually fallen, from the Observatory Hill and Sydney Airport gauges. Forecast models under-read storms.
  • NSW Beachwatch: the daily pollution forecast for each beach ("pollution likely", "possible"), and its yearly grades. When there's no forecast for the day, recent rain stands in.
  • Local knowledge: how each beach faces, what swell reaches it, ocean pool tides and cleaning days, gathered from councils, Beachsafe and surf guides.
  • Swell windows: measured from the OpenStreetMap coastline.
  • Map: Protomaps and OpenStreetMap.

It's a hobby project and forecasts are forecasts: check the flags and the water before you go in.
