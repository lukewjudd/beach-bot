About the data

How often

The bot runs every hour, at a quarter past, around the clock. The page picks up new scores on its own. The 6:15am run lands just after Beachwatch's daily update.

What a score means

Every day is cut into three sessions, the same every day of the week: morning (sunrise to 11am), midday (11am to 2pm) and evening (2pm to sunset). Every spot is scored out of 10 for each, on its best two hours, and a strip under the call shows every hour of the session, the best two ringed, so you can see if it turns (⚡ for storms). Warmth counts where the day's heading: a cool 7am on a day going to 30° is a warm-day swim, but not after a southerly change. 8/10 or better is worth it. 9/10 or better goes in the shared calendar (one event a day, up to two days ahead), and a 10/10 is every part at full marks: as good as it gets. Further out is an early forecast: a guide, not a plan. 0 means ruled out (pollution, storms, too rough, closed).

Swim or surf

Swimming and surfing are scored by two different models. A swim score wants calm, clean, warm water, and a surf score wants waves with some size and shape, so a great surf day can be a terrible swim. Settings (the cog, top right) picks which one the whole page follows: the week, the call, the bars, the list and the map. Swim is the default. A surf session is worth it at 8/10 on the surf scale, and pumping surf gets its own calendar event, never mixed in with the swim one. Settings also has the coast and units (wind in km/h, knots or mph, waves in metres or feet, °C or °F), and the page remembers them in your browser.

How swell and wind are worked out

The forecast describes the sea well offshore: a main swell, sometimes a second one, and chop from the local wind, each with a height, a direction and a period (the seconds between waves). What matters for a swim is what's left of that by the time it reaches the sand, and that's different at every beach. Here's how we get from one to the other.

First, two kinds of swell

Every wave out there was made by wind. Wind blowing over the sea nearby makes wind swell: short, steep waves a few seconds apart, often coming from more than one direction at once, so they arrive messy. A big storm far away makes groundswell. On its way here the waves sort themselves out: longer ones travel faster, leave the short ones behind and arrive first, as long, even lines well spaced apart. The period, the seconds between waves, tells you which you've got. Under about 8 seconds it's wind swell; past about 11, it's groundswell.

That's why period matters so much. Groundswell carries more energy for its height, it bends further round headlands, and it stands up taller when it reaches the sandbank or reef. A 1.5m wind swell is a choppy day; a 1.5m groundswell can be a very good one.

1. Find the open ocean

From just off each beach we draw a line every 2°, out to 30 km, over the OpenStreetMap coastline. Lines that make it all the way are open ocean: swell from those directions has a clear run in. Lines that hit land are blocked, usually by the beach's own headlands.

2. Let it bend

Swell isn't a beam of light. As it reaches shallower water it bends toward the shore, and some spreads sideways into the shadow behind a headland, so the edge of the window is a fade, not a cut. Long-period groundswell feels the bottom sooner and bends further: it wraps into corners a short wind swell can't reach. Swell that arrives side-on also spreads along more of the coast, which makes it smaller.

Bending also moves the energy around. The energy between two rays stays the same all the way in, so where rays bunch up the waves grow and where they spread apart the waves shrink. Rays bend onto the point, which gathers the swell in and gets the biggest waves, and fan out across the bay behind it, so the waves there are smaller and cleaner. That's why the corner of a beach can be calm when the middle is breaking, and why a groundswell can still have a sheltered corner breaking when a wind swell from the same direction leaves it flat.

3. Work out how much gets in

Put those together and every spot gets a share for each direction and period. The geometry is then checked against what locals already know about each beach (from surf guides, Beachsafe and councils): the two are blended, so the model can't stray far from what's known to happen. Harbour beaches have no open ocean to measure, so they go on local knowledge alone.

4. Size it up

Longer swell carries more energy for its height, so it breaks bigger. At a swimming beach the effect is gentle; at a surf break it's stronger, because a short, weak swell barely registers on a reef or bank.

Each wave train is worked out on its own direction, then they're added up by energy. So a big south groundswell can miss a beach that faces north-east while the east wind chop still gets in. The result is the height at the beach, which is what the calm-water and surf scores are based on.

5. Then the wind

Last comes the wind, and what matters most is which way it blows across the beach. Offshore wind comes off the land and out over the water: it flattens the surface, holds the waves up so they break cleanly, and keeps the water clear. Onshore wind comes in off the sea and piles chop on top of whatever swell is running, so even a small day turns messy and murky. Cross-shore sits in between.

Which direction counts as offshore depends on which way each beach faces, so it's worked out spot by spot. Strength comes first, though: under 8 km/h the water stays glassy whichever way it blows, and a light breeze is still pleasant from any side. Direction matters more the stronger it gets, and past 20 km/h it's blowy on the sand whichever way. Gusts of 35 km/h or more cost points too, whatever the wind.

6. Is the water clean?

Rain washes stormwater into the sea, and some beaches take it worse than others. If NSW Beachwatch has a forecast for the day, that's what counts: "pollution likely" rules a beach out, "possible" costs points. If not, the bot goes on the rain that's actually fallen. Each beach has its own limits, from its Beachwatch grades: open, well-flushed beaches shrug off a shower, enclosed ones don't.

Rain also clouds the water for snorkelling for a couple of days, so the snorkelling spots lose their "good snorkelling" bonus until it clears.

7. How warm will it feel?

A swim feels different at 19° and 29°. But a session's coldest hour isn't the whole story: a 7am dip on a day heading to 30° is a warm-day swim. So mornings count the warmth still to come (to early afternoon) and evenings keep the afternoon's, both 3° under, so only properly warm days get the full bonus. After a southerly change, an evening counts the temperature it actually is.

8. The sky

Storms rule a session out: lightning and water don't mix. Rain and heavy cloud cost points, warm air adds them. Water temperature only ever helps: warm water earns a few points, cool water is just noted (Sydney's water is cool much of the year, and that's no reason not to go). After a few days of north-easterlies the bot also warns that cold upwelling is likely.

9. Ocean pools and the tide

Pools stay flat when the beaches are rough, so they're scored differently: the walls keep the chop out, and what matters is big swell washing over them, which happens around high tide. Some pools are best at a particular tide (Wylie's and McIver's at low, Giles at mid), some are drained for cleaning one day a week, and the gated ones keep opening hours.

10. Bluebottles

From November to March, a day of north-easterlies or southerlies at 20 km/h or more can blow bluebottles onto the eastern beaches. Maroubra, open to both, cops it most; harbour beaches are spared. Where the lifeguards report on bluebottles that morning, their report counts instead (step 11).

11. What the lifeguards say

Everything so far is a forecast. At Clovelly, Coogee, Maroubra and Little Bay, Randwick's lifeguards also post a report each morning, usually by 8:30, and on the day their word beats the forecast. If they've closed a beach, it's out for the rest of the day (its ocean pools aren't: they have walls). If they've seen bluebottles, it loses the points whatever the wind's done, and if they've seen none, the wind-based guess is dropped. Their report shows under the beach's conditions for today's sessions, and their wave height is kept next to the swell model's so the model can be checked against it over time.

Where the numbers come from

  • Open-Meteo: wind, air temperature, rain, storms and UV, plus swell, sea temperature and tides from its marine forecast.
  • Bureau of Meteorology: rain that's actually fallen, from the Observatory Hill and Sydney Airport gauges. Forecast models under-read storms.
  • NSW Beachwatch: the daily pollution forecast for each beach ("pollution likely", "possible"), and its yearly grades. When there's no forecast for the day, recent rain stands in.
  • Randwick City Council lifeguard reports: each morning's beach status, waves, rips, bluebottles and water temperature at Clovelly, Coogee, Maroubra and Little Bay.
  • Local knowledge: how each beach faces, what swell reaches it, ocean pool tides and cleaning days, gathered from councils, Beachsafe and surf guides.
  • Wave physics: Rick Salmon, Introduction to Ocean Waves (Scripps Institution of Oceanography, UC San Diego): how swell sorts itself out on the way from a distant storm, wave speed by depth, refraction and the energy between rays.
  • Swell windows: measured from the OpenStreetMap coastline.
  • Map: Protomaps and OpenStreetMap.
