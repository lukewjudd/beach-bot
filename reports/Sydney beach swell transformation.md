# Replace hand-set sectors with fitted transfer tables

The best approach for this bot keeps its current structure: each swell train goes through its own exposure function, the trains are combined as energy, and per-beach corrections are fitted to observations. That is the same architecture commercial forecasters describe. What should change is where the numbers come from. Each beach's response to direction and period should be taken from the official NSW Nearshore Wave Tool, which pre-computes WAVEWATCH III transfers at **10,679 points spaced 250 m apart along the 10 m and 30 m contours**, and was validated at Narrabeen, Collaroy, Bronte and Maroubra with Hs RMSE of about **0.24–0.31 m** ([Baird/DCCEEW validation report](https://www.environment.nsw.gov.au/sites/default/files/2025-08/nsw-nearshore-wave-tool-validation-report.pdf)). Hand-set NE/E/SE/S coefficients should stop being the source. Physics changes two more things. Swell-window edges should be soft tanh steps that give about 0.71 of the height exactly at the geometric edge. The flat 1.3x boost for 12 s+ swell should become a continuous period factor, because the classic breaker-height relation scales as **T^0.4**. That is about 1.12x at 12 s and 1.26x at 16 s relative to 9 s, so the boost is too large at 12 s and too sudden. Long-period "wrap" into sheltered corners comes mainly from refraction, not diffraction. It belongs in the direction response, where a transfer table captures it naturally, and not in a size multiplier. Harbour beaches and the alongshore differences within a beach (Ben Buckler, Barrenjoey) are not covered by any published source. They stay as hand-set priors until observations correct them.

## Your current design already matches the industry pattern

No commercial forecaster publishes its spot formulas, but their public descriptions converge. Surf Captain runs a WW3-based model, takes one offshore output point per location, splits the spectrum into its two most energetic swells, estimates how much of each reaches the spot, and feeds both into a proprietary algorithm ([Surf Captain](https://surfcaptain.com/blog/1/understand-the-surf-height-forecast)). Surfline's LOTUS tunes each spot from forecaster reports, surfer feedback and cam observations, using machine learning to decide how far to adjust the model ([Surfline LOTUS](https://www.surfline.com/lp/whatsnew/features/lotus-swell-model)). Even after this, Surfline's published Del Mar figures put the 4–6 ft forecast within 1.5 ft only **about 62% of the time**. The base model also undercalled long-period NW swell by roughly 2 ft before tuning, which is a bias that depends on both direction and period. Brazil's Surfguru applies Komar & Gaughan to WW3 output, calls the result "very generic", and corrects it with surfer reports ([Surfguru](https://surfguru.com.br/conteudo/estimating-wave-height-at-surf-spots-2024-07-05-29190.html)). The open-source landscape is thin: meta-surf-forecast plots "swell height × period × 0.1" ([meta-surf-forecast](https://github.com/swrobel/meta-surf-forecast)). A bot with per-partition directional exposure is therefore already ahead of most public code.

The Surfline example carries a specific lesson. If each beach gets a single multiplier, or a multiplier that depends only on direction, the leftover error still has a pattern by direction × period. The bot's current sector coefficients plus a separate period boost are a factorised approximation of what should be a two-dimensional table, k(θ, T). Sydney's swell mix makes this matter. Mid-shelf wave climates cluster into easterly (Tradewind), south-easterly (Tasman) and southerly (Southern Ocean) types with distinct power, and the mix has been rotating anticlockwise toward E and SE ([Mortlock & Goodwin 2015](https://researchers.mq.edu.au/en/publications/directional-wave-climate-and-power-variability-along-the-southeas/)). Each type has its own typical period, so direction and period are correlated in the data, and treating them separately mis-scores beaches in a structured way.

Three operational pitfalls apply to the inputs. First, Open-Meteo ranks partitions by energy, so "primary" can be a south groundswell one hour and an east swell the next ([Meteomatics on partition ranking](https://www.meteomatics.com/en/api/available-parameters/marine-parameters/wave-height/)). The latest commit, which runs each wave train through its own direction for swim beaches, is the right fix and should apply to every beach. Second, Open-Meteo says secondary swell is "only available for some models", and its 0.16° GFS Wave domain stops at 15°S, so it does not cover Sydney. It also warns that "accuracy at coastal areas is limited" ([Open-Meteo marine API](https://open-meteo.com/en/docs/marine-weather-api)). Pin one model, such as `ecmwf_wam025` or MFWAM, so calibration coefficients don't shift silently when "best match" changes backend. Third, query one exposed offshore node, roughly 10–20 km off the coast near the MHL Sydney buoy, for every beach, the way surf-forecast.com picks an "optimum grid node" per break ([surf-forecast.com](https://www.surf-forecast.com/breaks/The-Spot/surf-stats)). A grid cell nearest a beach may already sit in a headland shadow, which would count the sheltering twice.

## Long-period wrap comes from refraction, not diffraction

Linear theory makes the period effect concrete. Over straight, parallel contours, H = H0·Ks·Kr, with shoaling Ks = sqrt(cg0/cg), refraction Kr = sqrt(cos θ0/cos θ), and direction following Snell's law ([Apsley, Manchester notes](https://personalpages.manchester.ac.uk/staff/david.d.apsley/lectures/hydraulics3/slidesWaveTransformation.pdf)). The research notes computed values for swell arriving 45° off the beach normal. At 10 m depth, a 6 s swell has only turned to 37.5°, while a 14 s swell has turned to **18° off shore-normal** and has been shoaled about 21% more (Ks 1.11 vs 0.91). This is why long-period south swell finds its way into NE-facing corners: it bends toward the beach well offshore.

Diffraction behaves the opposite way, which is counter-intuitive. For directional random seas, Goda showed that penetration into a headland's geometric shadow depends mainly on the directional spreading parameter smax. The recommended deep-water values are **10 for wind waves, 25 for short swell and 75 for long, low-steepness swell** ([USACE CETN-I-18](https://erdc-library.erdc.dren.mil/server/api/core/bitstreams/81b728f8-7039-4ef8-e053-411ac80adeb3/content)). Clean, narrow-spread groundswell spreads into the shadow *less* than messy wind sea does. Kraus (1984) fitted Goda's diagrams with a closed form that suits per-beach code. Energy factor E(θ) = 0.5·[tanh(smax·θ/W) + 1], height factor K = sqrt(E), and W = −0.000130·smax² + 0.270·smax + 5.31. Here θ is the signed angle in radians from the window edge, positive on the open side. The fit matches Goda well except where K < 0.2, where it underestimates ([CETN-I-18](https://erdc-library.erdc.dren.mil/server/api/core/bitstreams/81b728f8-7039-4ef8-e053-411ac80adeb3/content)).

| Angle from window edge | smax 10 (wind sea) | smax 25 (short swell) | smax 75 (groundswell) |
|---|---|---|---|
| +30° (open side) | 0.89 | 0.95 | 0.98 |
| +15° | 0.81 | 0.87 | 0.91 |
| 0° (geometric edge) | 0.71 | 0.71 | 0.71 |
| −15° (into shadow) | 0.59 | 0.50 | 0.41 |
| −30° | 0.46 | 0.32 | 0.20 |
| −60° | 0.26 | 0.11 | 0.04 |

*Height ratios computed from Kraus's fit. The transition half-widths are about 46°, 27° and 19° respectively.*

The table tells you how a window edge should behave. A hard cut is wrong. A cosine taper that gives 1.0 at the edge is too generous. The physically grounded value at the edge is **0.71**. Wind chop leaks furthest past a headland, but it is weak to begin with. A clean groundswell has a sharp diffraction edge, but refraction has already shifted where its edge sits. In code, apply Snell's law from deep water to a reference depth of about 10–15 m before evaluating the tanh step. That moves a long-period swell's effective direction toward the beach normal, so its window widens. Keep in mind that a Sydney headland is not a thin breakwater. Reflection and reef refraction around the tip add energy, so Kraus's values are a lower bound on how much energy leaks into the shadow.

The breaker-height relation decides the size boost. Komar & Gaughan (1972) gives Hb = 0.39·g^(1/5)·(T·H0²)^(2/5), which works out to **Hb ≈ 0.62·H0^0.8·T^0.4** in SI units ([Komar & Gaughan, ICCE](https://icce-ojs-tamu.tdl.org/icce/article/view/2763)). Going from 8 s to 14 s raises breaker height by about 1.25x. Relative to a 9 s reference, the factor is 0.85 at 6 s, 1.04 at 10 s, 1.12 at 12 s, 1.19 at 14 s and 1.26 at 16 s. The bot's current step of 1.3x at 12 s therefore over-credits marginal 12 s swell and gives nothing to an 11 s swell. The H0^0.8 term also means small swells get relatively more amplification than large ones. Surfers' period-multiplier tables are usually steeper than T^0.4, but no reputable primary source for them could be traced ([Swellnet notes on 12→16 s growth](https://swellnet.com/forums/swellnet-forecast-notes/88181) is qualitative). Treat the exponent as a parameter you can fit, starting at 0.4 and perhaps reaching 0.5–0.7 if observations demand it.

## Narrabeen's 30% reversal anchors Sydney-specific numbers

Sydney has two open, quantitative transformation datasets, and both are better than hand-tuning. The NSW Nearshore Wave Tool (nearshore.waves.nsw.gov.au) was rebuilt by Baird for DCCEEW in 2024. It uses WW3 v6.07 on an unstructured mesh refined to about 150 m at the 10 m contour, with new NSW Marine LiDAR bathymetry. Its transfer matrices span **25 log-spaced periods from 3.6 to 24.3 s and offshore directions from 0 to 230°TN in 5° steps**, and include a 67-year ERA5-driven hindcast and a 10-day forecast ([validation report](https://www.environment.nsw.gov.au/sites/default/files/2025-08/nsw-nearshore-wave-tool-validation-report.pdf)). Per-point forecast and hindcast CSVs can be downloaded, and a data-request form emails NetCDF/CSV for up to 10 points per request. The raw transfer matrices are not published. The JSON API behind the app needs a session token and is undocumented, so ask DCCEEW before automating it. No licence statement for the outputs was found. The report also notes that peak direction validates far worse than height (θp RMSE 18–34°), and that the Sydney offshore buoy sits 20–25 km NNE of Bronte and Maroubra. That distance adds directional error when the buoy is the input, so lean on the tool's Hs ratios rather than its nearshore directions.

The Narrabeen–Collaroy dataset is the most open per-beach transfer. Turner et al. (2016) transformed the Sydney buoy record to 10 m depth at five profiles using a **1,573-run SWAN lookup table**: Hs 0.25–9.5 m, Tp 2–17 s, directions 0–245°TN. They shipped it as a commented MATLAB function under CC BY, with data on Dryad. Validation against an inshore buoy gave Hs R = 0.94 and RMSE 0.18 m ([Turner et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4828941/); [Dryad](https://datadryad.org/stash/dataset/doi:10.5061/dryad.28g01)). The same paper gives the only sourced Sydney number for headland sheltering. Long Reef shelters the south (Collaroy) end, so modelled breakers are **about 30% larger at the north end under average southerly conditions, and about 30% larger at the south end under NE waves**. Direction also controls damage: in the June 2016 East Coast Low, the anomalous E–NE storm direction, not height alone, drove the worst Narrabeen erosion in about four decades ([Mortlock et al. 2017](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4441/9/2/121); [Harley et al. 2017 summary](https://riskfrontiers.com/?p=2876)).

For observations, three sources are worth using. The MHL Sydney Waverider sits off Long Reef at about 85 m depth, has been directional since 1992, and is archived on AODN THREDDS as WAVESYD. Its licence terms are inconsistent across listings, ranging from CC BY 4.0 to "not specified" ([data.gov.au](https://data.gov.au/data/dataset/000cb6d7-dea9-4e3a-869e-08f99e5f3434)). DCCEEW's nearshore buoy deployments include Bronte (14 m, Aug–Oct 2016) and Maroubra (Aug–Oct 2016) under CC BY ([data.nsw.gov.au](https://data.nsw.gov.au/data/dataset/13dcfe36-a8ad-42ad-ad48-5734897df4c7)). Pairing those with the Sydney buoy gives a short but real measured check on eastern-suburbs ratios. No council coastal management program or hazard study with per-beach nearshore tables was found for Waverley, Randwick or the Northern Beaches; the Waverley scoping study PDF was not accessible ([Waverley Council](https://www.waverley.nsw.gov.au/media/documents/environment/Waverley_Beaches_CMP_Stage_1_-_Scoping_Study_final.pdf)). Common local claims, such as Ben Buckler shadowing north Bondi from the NE, Barrenjoey sheltering north Palm Beach, and the size of the Wedding Cake Island effect at Coogee, could not be verified. Treat them as priors, not facts. Harbour beaches such as Camp Cove and Nielsen Park are probably outside the tool's reliable coverage. For them, a heavy reduction that only lets through large, long-period E–ENE swell entering the Heads is a sensible assumption, but it has no source behind it.

## A four-stage pipeline the bot can run

The recommended model transforms each Open-Meteo partition i (primary swell, secondary swell, wind wave) separately, using its own height H_i, direction θ_i and period T_i, before combining them.

| Stage | Computation | Source of numbers |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Exposure | K_i = table lookup k_beach(θ_i, T_i) if available; otherwise window K = sqrt(0.5[tanh(smax·θ_edge/W)+1]) × sqrt(cos θ_rel), with θ refracted to ~10–15 m depth | NSW tool hindcast ratios; Kraus/Goda fallback |
| 2. Breaker height | Hb_i = 0.62·(K_i·H_i)^0.8·T_i^0.4 | Komar & Gaughan |
| 3. Combine | Hb = sqrt(Σ Hb_i²), optionally weighting the longest-period train | Spectral variance additivity ([Oceanweather](https://oceanweather.com/about/FIELDSDEF-Website.pdf)) |
| 4. Calibrate | Hb_final = k_spot·Hb, with k_spot shrunk toward a regional prior | Observations (next section) |

The fastest win for stage 1 is to build the per-beach table from the NSW tool. Pick the 10 m-contour point nearest each beach, request the hindcast for 10 points at a time, and pair it hour by hour with the tool's offshore input or the Sydney buoy. Then bin Hs_near/Hs_off into roughly 10° × 2 s cells. Baird's earlier sensitivity work found the parametric transfer coefficients insensitive to Hs until waves approach breaking at 10 m, so a ratio table fitted this way should closely reproduce the official matrix ([validation report §3.2.3](https://www.environment.nsw.gov.au/sites/default/files/2025-08/nsw-nearshore-wave-tool-validation-report.pdf)). For Narrabeen–Collaroy, the Turner MATLAB function already gives the same thing at five alongshore positions. Where a beach has strong differences along its length, such as Bondi, Manly–Shelly and Palm Beach, sample two or three points (sheltered corner, middle, exposed end) instead of one average. The Narrabeen ±30% reversal shows why one number per beach hides a lot.

If the tool's data proves hard to get, or for harbour beaches, the fallback is geometric. Cast rays every 1–2° from a point 200–500 m seaward of the beach against OSM land polygons in a metric projection (EPSG:28356). Mark a bearing open if the ray reaches 50–200 km without hitting land, then soften the edges with the tanh law. InVEST's coastal-vulnerability module does this kind of fetch ray-casting and is a ready template ([InVEST docs](https://invest.readthedocs.io/en/3.7.0/ecosystem_service_analysis_tools/coastal_vulnerability.html)). Map smax from the partition: about 10 for wind waves and T ≤ 7 s, 25 for 8–11 s, and 50–75 for T ≥ 12 s low-steepness swell. These period thresholds are a heuristic, not sourced values. Wind waves must go through the same function with their own direction. WAM labels building NE sea under an onshore wind as wind wave, so dropping that partition would underpredict NE windswell days at NE-facing beaches. Energy below about 6 s can be downweighted, following StormSurf's cutoff at 5.88 s ([StormSurf FAQ](https://www.stormsurf.com/buoy/mht/faqs.html)).

## Calibrate in two stages, with shrinkage

Separate the two error sources first. Compare Open-Meteo's offshore Hs, Tp and direction with the MHL Sydney buoy, so that model error is not blamed on the beach transfer. Swellnet's forecasters report "poor correlation between buoy data and surf size" for Sydney south swells ([Swellnet notes](https://swellnet.com/reports/forecaster-notes/sydney-hunter-illawarra/2018/02/16/strong-swells-entire-period-biggest)). Directions of 160–190° are where exposure differs most between beaches and where model direction error matters most. Concentrate calibration effort there. Then compare predicted beach heights against observed reports. The best sources are your own logged observations or labelled cam screenshots, followed by lifeguard or council reports. Visual breaker observations remain the reference standard for surf verification ([Caldwell 2004](https://data-ww3.ifremer.fr/BIB/Caldwell_8WIF2004.pdf)).

Fit log(H_obs) = log(k_spot) + log(H_pred) plus a log offset for each source. The offset absorbs conventions such as face height vs Hawaiian scale vs Hs, which can bias k by 1.5–2x ([Surfresearch on wave-height conventions](https://surfresearch.com.au/awaveheight.html)). Shrink each beach toward a regional mean: log k_spot = (n/σ²·mean_log_ratio + μ/τ²)/(n/σ² + 1/τ²). With this, a beach with three reports stays near the prior and one with fifty follows its own data. Your current hand-set coefficients make a good prior μ. Once a beach has about 30 observations, add direction bins (S 150–190°, SE 110–150°, E–NE 45–110°) before adding period terms. Track MAE, hit rate within ±0.3 m, and bias by direction bin, in line with Surfline's own within-1.5 ft metric. The shrinkage scheme and the bin edges are design recommendations, not documented hobbyist practice.

## Conclusion

The current design is close to right. The main limit is that it is hand-tuned, while the state of NSW has already paid for a validated direction × period transfer to the 10 m contour off nearly every open Sydney beach. The decisive upgrade is replacing four sector numbers and a period step with an empirical k(θ, T) table derived from that tool, which also captures the refraction-driven wrap that the 1.3x boost was approximating. Observations then only need to correct what the tool cannot know: surf-zone breaking, sandbanks, local reefs and the beach's reporting conventions. Physics formulas (Kraus edges, Komar–Gaughan) serve as the fallback and as a check that the table behaves sensibly.

Two uncertainties remain. The NSW tool's data licence and automated access are unresolved, and harbour beaches and alongshore corners have no published transfer at all. Ask DCCEEW and MHL directly about reuse terms before building on their data. Until then, use local knowledge as an explicitly labelled prior that observations are allowed to overrule.
