# Offshore wind strength, gusts and swell: evidence for a better wind rule

Researched 9 Nov 2026. The question: the current rule gives offshore wind full marks at ≤12 km/h,
cuts it to 40% above 20 km/h, and penalises gusts ≥40 km/h. That underrated Maroubra on 12 Apr 2026
(4-6 ft clean east groundswell, ~11 s, W offshore 21 km/h gusting ~49 km/h, spray off the lip).
What do the physics and the practitioners say about how strong an offshore can get before it hurts,
and does that depend on swell size and period?

Paywalled papers were read through abstracts, open-access copies, or how later open-access papers
describe them. Each entry says which.

---

## Short answer

1. **Physics: offshore wind changes the breaker shape in the direction surfers want. In the only
   field-scale measurement, the benefit levels off at a fairly light wind relative to the wave's
   speed. None of the studies found the shape getting worse as offshore wind got stronger.** Lab
   flumes ran winds of about ±5 times the wave speed and still got later, shallower, plunging breakers
   under offshore wind. The Surf Ranch field study found the overturn area stops growing once the
   offshore wind is about 0.4 times the breaking-wave speed.
2. **The penalty for strong offshore is practical, not about wave shape:** it is harder to paddle in,
   the lip holds up so the take-off comes late and steep, spray blinds you, small waves may not break
   at all, and gusts chop up the face. Practitioners agree on this, but almost nobody publishes numbers.
   The few who do put "still good" at up to about 15 kn (28 km/h) and "experienced surfers only" above that.
3. **How much wind is "too much" scales with how fast the wave is moving.** The science measures wind
   as U/C (wind speed divided by breaking-wave speed). Bigger waves, which longer periods produce for a
   given offshore height, move faster, so the same 21 km/h is relatively weaker on a 2 m groundswell
   than on a 0.8 m windswell. Opposing-wind lab work also found steep (short-period) waves lose more
   energy to opposing wind than gentle long-period swell does. This is the evidence-based reason to
   let the offshore tolerance grow with swell size and period.
4. **Maroubra 12 Apr 2026 worked out:** a breaking wave of about 2.2 m moves at about 5.3 m/s. A
   21 km/h wind (5.8 m/s) gives U/C ≈ 1.1, only a little beyond the field-tested range (up to 1.2)
   and well inside the lab range (about 5). Gusts of 49 km/h give U/C ≈ 2.6, still inside the lab
   range. On the evidence the session should score near the top for wind, not 40%.

## 1. Physics of wind on shoaling and breaking waves

### Douglass & Weggel (1988), Douglass (1990)
- **Citation:** Douglass, S.L. & Weggel, J.R. (1988) *Laboratory experiments on the influence of wind
  on nearshore wave breaking*. Proc. 21st ICCE, 46. https://icce-ojs-tamu.tdl.org/icce/article/view/4253 ;
  Douglass, S.L. (1990) *Influence of wind on breaking waves*. J. Waterway, Port, Coastal & Ocean Eng.
  116(6), 651-663. https://ascelibrary.com/doi/10.1061/%28ASCE%290733-950X%281990%29116%3A6%28651%29
  (paywalled; abstract plus later papers' descriptions). The PhD thesis (Drexel 1989) is open:
  https://researchdiscovery.drexel.edu/esploro/outputs/doctoral/991021888769504721
- **What it found:** In a wind-wave flume, onshore wind made waves break earlier, in deeper water, and
  spill. Offshore wind made them break later, in shallower water, and plunge. Swinging the wind from
  offshore to onshore made the surf zone up to 100% wider. The wind mattered most for waves that were
  near the spill/plunge boundary to begin with. Breaker *height* changed little, breaker *depth*
  changed a lot. Wind shear, not normal pressure, was suggested as the main cause. Feddersen et al.
  (2023) put the lab winds at about U/C ≈ ±5, far stronger relative to the waves than anything you
  see at a beach.
- **For the rule:** Offshore wind's main effect is a later, shallower, more plunging break, and no
  upper limit showed up even at very strong relative winds. Sydney beach breaks on moderate slopes sit
  near the spill/plunge boundary, which is where Douglass found the wind matters most. So the
  direction of the wind matters more than any speed limit for the wave itself.

### King & Baker (1996)
- **Citation:** King, D.M. & Baker, C.J. (1996) *Changes to wave parameters in the surf zone due to
  wind effects*. J. Hydraulic Research 34(1). https://iahr.org/library/info?pid=6053 (abstract and
  secondary descriptions; King's 1994 Nottingham PhD covers the same work).
- **What it found:** Scale-model waves (about 1:50) on a 1:20 beach, in a wind tunnel combined with a
  wave tank. The results matched Douglass: offshore wind meant later, plunging breaking and a narrower
  surf zone, onshore meant earlier spilling. Breaking height was largely unchanged, but because the
  break depth moved, the height-to-depth ratio at breaking changed: higher under offshore wind.
- **For the rule:** A second independent lab gets the same direction and the same lack of an upper
  limit.

### Feddersen & Veron (2005)
- **Citation:** Feddersen, F. & Veron, F. (2005) *Wind effects on shoaling wave shape*. J. Phys.
  Oceanogr. 35(7), 1223-1228. https://data-ww3.ifremer.fr/BIB/Feddersen_Verron_JPO2005.pdf
- **What it found:** Monochromatic waves with *onshore* wind in a lab tank. The wind added energy at
  the harmonics and changed skewness and asymmetry, giving more pitched-forward waves. Cross-shore wind
  strongly changed the breakpoint and breaking height. Offshore wind was not tested.
- **For the rule:** Supporting evidence that wind changes wave shape before breaking, not just the
  surface texture. Says nothing about strong offshore wind.

### Sous et al. (2021)
- Lab study of wind on shoaling and breaking. I only saw it through later papers' descriptions: onshore
  wind gives earlier breaking in deeper water, and a parameterised wind energy input in a wave-averaged
  model reproduced the shift in breakpoint and H/h at breaking. It is described alongside
  skewness/asymmetry changes like Feddersen & Veron's. No wind speeds were available to me.
- **For the rule:** Same direction as the others. Treat it as corroboration only.

### Zdyrski & Feddersen (2022), theory
- **Citation:** Zdyrski, T. & Feddersen, F. (2022) *Wind-induced changes to shoaling surface gravity
  wave shape*. Phys. Rev. Fluids 7, 074802. https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.05519
- **What it found:** In a weakly nonlinear model with wind-induced surface pressure, offshore wind
  *weakly* raises the pre-breaking height/depth ratio and face slope, while onshore wind strongly
  narrows the peak. The wind's effect on how wide the pre-breaking zone is grows on gentler beach slopes.
- **For the rule:** On these beach breaks the offshore effect is real but modest in the model. The
  onshore effect is stronger, which supports penalising onshore wind harder than rewarding offshore.

### Feddersen, Fincham, Brodie et al. (2023), field scale at Surf Ranch
- **Citation:** Feddersen, F. et al. (2023) *Cross-shore wind-induced changes to field-scale
  overturning wave shape*. J. Fluid Mech. 958, A4. doi:10.1017/jfm.2023.40 (open access).
  https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-fluid-mechanics/article/crossshore-windinduced-changes-to-fieldscale-overturning-wave-shape/4236FD21F9A9EA5AFCF6ED8966B93596
  ; press summary: https://today.ucsd.edu/story/scientists-and-surf-organizations-confirm-what-surfers-already-know
- **What it found:** 22 surfing-sized waves of about 2.25 m, moving at 6.7 m/s, scanned with lidar.
  Wind was measured at 16 m: about 3-7 m/s on two days and 0.5-2 m/s on the third. The relative wind
  ran from U/C = −1.2 (offshore) to +0.7 (onshore).
  - Breakpoint location tracked U/C closely (r ≈ 0.83): offshore wind meant later breaking.
  - Overturn area (A/H²) and aspect ratio (width/length) were both bigger and squarer under offshore
    wind. Typical W/L was about 0.5 offshore against 0.35 onshore, and the overturn parameters varied
    by about a factor of two across the observed winds, an effect as large as big changes in H/h.
  - **Under stronger offshore wind (U/C below about −0.4), the overturn area levelled off at about
    A/H² ≈ 0.4.** It did not grow any further, and it did not shrink either, up to U/C ≈ −1.2.
  - The authors put this in ocean terms: a wave breaking in 2.5 m of water moves at C ≈ 5 m/s, so the
    observed range is about −5 to +3.5 m/s of cross-shore wind (−18 to +13 km/h).
  - They infer that offshore wind gives narrower surf zones and more energetic overturns.
- **For the rule:**
  - The shape benefit is already complete at a light offshore of about U/C ≈ 0.4, roughly 7-9 km/h for
    beach-break waves. Above that, more wind buys no better shape.
  - Up to U/C ≈ 1.2, about 20-30 km/h for solid surf, the shape did not get worse either. So between
    "light" and "fresh" offshore the score should hold close to full and come down only for practical
    reasons.
  - **This is the strongest evidence that the current 40% cut at 20 km/h is too steep for solid swell.**

### Feddersen, Hanson, Mostert & Fincham (2024), simulation
- **Citation:** *Modelling wind-induced changes to overturning wave shape*. J. Fluid Mech. 1000, A48.
  doi:10.1017/jfm.2024.1012 (open access). https://falk.ucsd.edu/pdf/Feddersen_etal2024JFM.pdf
- **What it found:** In 2-D two-phase simulations of a solitary wave on steep bathymetry under strong
  onshore and offshore wind, offshore wind again gave later breaking and a bigger overturn. The
  aspect-ratio trend came out the opposite way to the experiment, and the simulation needed a stronger
  wind than the field to get the same effect, which the authors put down to lab-scale Reynolds and Bond
  numbers. The mechanism is low pressure in the lee of the crest.
- **For the rule:** Confirms the direction of the breakpoint and overturn-size effects. It does not
  give usable real-world thresholds.

### Peirson, Garcia & Pells (2003), opposing wind
- **Citation:** *Water wave attenuation due to opposing wind*. J. Fluid Mech. 487, 345-365.
  https://data-ww3.ifremer.fr/BIB/Peirson_etal_JFM2003.pdf
- **What it found:** In the lab, opposing wind attenuated waves faster than expected, about 2.5 times
  the growth rate under a comparable following wind. The loss became important once wave steepness
  passed a critical value. Mitsuyasu & Yoshida (2005) later measured smaller rates.
- **For the rule:** A strong offshore wind takes more out of steep, short-period waves than gentle
  long-period groundswell. That backs giving long-period swell more tolerance, as a qualitative
  direction only, since over the last few hundred metres the size loss is small. Swellnet readers
  report the same thing anecdotally (strong offshores "flatten" short-period swell more):
  https://www.swellnet.com/comment/726923

### What the physics does *not* cover
None of these studies deals with what spoils surfing under a strong offshore: paddling into a wave
against the wind, spray off the lip, the board catching air on the drop, small waves that won't break,
or gust-driven ripple and catspaws on the face. Those are the real limits, and they come only from
practitioners.

## 2. Surfing-science literature and surfability indices

- **Espejo, Losada & Méndez (2014)** *Surfing wave climate variability*. Global and Planetary
  Change 121, 19-25. doi:10.1016/j.gloplacha.2014.06.006.
  https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0921818114001192 (paywalled; abstract only).
  This is the Global Surf Index: an expert-judgement composite of wave height, period, wind, tide and
  SST at a resolution of about 100 km. Its wind component is in the paywalled methods, and **I could
  not confirm its thresholds**, so don't cite numbers from it. At that resolution it can't see local
  wind direction relative to a single beach in any detail anyway.
- **Mead & Black (2001)** *Predicting the breaking intensity of surfing waves* and *Functional
  component combinations controlling surfing wave quality at world-class surfing breaks*, J. Coastal
  Research SI 29. https://ref.coastalrestorationtrust.org.nz/documents/predicting-the-breaking-intensity-of-surfing-waves/
  These predict breaking intensity (vortex ratio) from the seabed gradient along the wave orthogonal.
  Wind is not a predictor. They argue that bathymetry sets how hollow a break can get, and Douglass's
  work suggests offshore wind pushes a beach break toward that limit.
- **Scarfe, Healy & Rennie (2009)** *Research-based surfing literature for coastal management and the
  science of surfing: a review*. J. Coastal Research 25(3), 539-557. https://www.doi.org/10.2112/07-0958.1 ;
  companion overview: https://ref.coastalrestorationtrust.org.nz/site/assets/files/7294/4__scarfe_healy_rennie_and_mead.pdf
  They treat wind only qualitatively: offshore is favourable, and wind climate is something to record
  for a break. No speed thresholds.
- **Boqué Ciurana & Aguilar (2020)** *Expected distribution of surfing days in the Iberian Peninsula*.
  J. Mar. Sci. Eng. 8(8), 599. https://www.mdpi.com/2077-1312/8/8/599 . They define a surfing day by
  swell direction and Hs > 0.9 m, and characterise each spot's optimal wind direction. They describe
  calm "glassy" as the best wind state. No offshore speed limit was visible in the abstract.
- **Somo (Cantabria) surf-climate indices**, *Sustainability* 14(14), 8496 (2022).
  https://www.mdpi.com/2071-1050/14/14/8496 . Surfing-day indices broken down by skill level, built
  from high-resolution wind and wave data. The full text was blocked to me, so the wind thresholds are
  unconfirmed.

**Bottom line for section 2:** The academic surfability indices use wind as a direction filter plus a
loose "light is best". I found none that publishes a "too strong offshore" threshold or a wind
tolerance that depends on swell size or period. The size dependence has to come from the physics
scaling (U/C) and from practitioners.

Where the practitioners agree: up to about 15 kn (28 km/h) offshore is still good, beginners

widely held but rarely written down. Surfnerd and the Aljezur guide come closest ("short-period swell
suffers more", "the more favourable the direction, the stronger it can be").

## 4. Sydney / NSW specifics

- **Westerlies are the winter-half offshore for east-facing beaches.** In the Bureau of Meteorology's
  winter 2009 summary, coastal Sydney had SW-NW winds about 67% of the time against a normal 59%
  (https://www.bom.gov.au/climate/current/season/nsw/archive/200908.sydney.shtml). The westerlies come
  from the subtropical ridge sitting north of Sydney and blow dry air off the continent. NE sea breezes
  dominate late spring to early autumn.
- **Practical consequences** (my judgement, consistent with the Swellnet notes):
  - Autumn/winter W-NW mornings over E-SE groundswell are the classic set-up for Maroubra, Bronte and
    Bondi south.
  - Those westerlies are often fresh and gusty, because they come down dry off the land and mix down
    the stronger winds above, and they tend to ease or swing as the day warms.
  - A 10 m model wind of 20-25 km/h gusting 40-50 is common on exactly these days. A rule that marks
    it down to 40% will underrate the region's best season.
  - Forecasts at grid scale may overstate the wind in the surf zone at beaches backed by dunes or
    headlands, such as Maroubra's south end and Bronte. That is a reason to be lenient, not harsh, at
    moderate offshore speeds.

## 5. Recommended rule

### Core idea
Score offshore wind by **relative wind r = U_off / C_b**, where:
- U_off is the onshore/offshore component of the 10 m wind in m/s;
- C_b ≈ √(g·h_b) is the breaking-wave speed, with h_b = H_b / 0.78;
- the breaking height comes from Komar & Gaughan: H_b ≈ 0.39·g^0.2·(T·H₀²)^0.4, using offshore Hs
  (H₀) and peak period (T). This brings period in automatically: longer period gives a bigger,
  faster breaker, which tolerates more wind.

This is the dimensionless number the lab and field studies use. If you'd rather not compute it, use
the lookup table below, which comes from it.

Example breaking-wave speeds:

| Swell class | Offshore Hs × Tp | H_b | C_b |
|---|---|---|---|
| Small / short | 0.6 m × 8 s | ~0.9 m | ~3.4 m/s |
| Moderate | 1.0 m × 10 s | ~1.5 m | ~4.4 m/s |
| Solid groundswell | 1.5 m × 11 s (Maroubra 12 Apr) | ~2.2 m | ~5.3 m/s |
| Big groundswell | 2.5 m × 13 s | ~3.6 m | ~6.7 m/s |

### Offshore score as a fraction of full wind points, f(r)

| r = U_off / C_b | Fraction | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| calm, U < 5 km/h | 0.95 | Glassy is classic. The field study measured slightly smaller overturns than under light offshore. |
| 0 < r ≤ 0.8 | 1.00 | The shape benefit is complete by r ≈ 0.4 (Feddersen 2023). Paddling is unaffected. |
| 0.8 → 1.3 | 1.00 → 0.85, linear | Field data show no loss of shape up to r ≈ 1.2. Paddling effort starts to rise (practitioners). |
| 1.3 → 2.0 | 0.85 → 0.60, linear | Beyond the field-tested range. Late take-offs and spray. |
| 2.0 → 3.0 | 0.60 → 0.35, linear | The lab still shows plunging at r ≈ 5, but the session is hard work. |
| > 3.0 | 0.35 → 0.25 floor at r ≥ 3.5 | Hard to catch waves; small swell may not break. |

### The same rule as a lookup table (sustained offshore component, 10 m, km/h)

The cells are f(r) at roughly each band's midpoint. The formula is smoother: for example, 21 km/h on
solid groundswell gives 0.91, while the 20-28 band average is 0.85.

| Offshore wind | Small/short (Hs<0.8 m or Tp<8 s) | Moderate (0.8-1.5 m, 8-11 s) | Solid groundswell (≥1.5 m, ≥10 s) | Big (≥2.5 m, ≥12 s) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0-5 (glassy) | 0.95 | 0.95 | 0.95 | 0.95 |
| 5-12 | 1.00 | 1.00 | 1.00 | 1.00 |
| 12-20 | 0.85 | 0.95 | 1.00 | 1.00 |
| 20-28 | 0.65 | 0.80 | 0.85 | 0.95 |
| 28-36 | 0.45 | 0.60 | 0.75 | 0.85 |
| 36-45 | 0.30 | 0.45 | 0.60 | 0.70 |
| > 45 | 0.25 | 0.30 | 0.40 | 0.55 |

### Gusts
- **Offshore gusts:** the physics shows no harm to wave shape at these speeds, so the cost of gusts is
  practical (paddling, spray, ripple on the face). Work out r_g = G_off / C_b:
  - r_g ≤ 3: no extra penalty;
  - 3 < r_g ≤ 4: ×0.85;
  - r_g > 4: ×0.70.

  Maroubra 12 Apr: r_g = 13.6 / 5.3 ≈ 2.6, so no gust penalty. A 49 km/h gust on a 0.8 m windswell
  (C ≈ 3.4) gives r_g ≈ 4.0, which takes the ×0.85.
- **Very strong wind in any direction:** if the sustained wind is ≥ 45 km/h or gusts are ≥ 70 km/h,
  cap the fraction at 0.3. This follows Surf-forecast's "very strong wind = 0" rule, softened for
  sheltered corners.
- **Onshore and cross-onshore gusts:** keep the existing gust penalty. There gusts do damage the wave
  itself, by adding whitecaps and chop.

### Cross-shore
Split the wind into its offshore component U_off = U·cos θ (θ = angle from the beach's offshore
bearing) and its alongshore component V = U·|sin θ|.
- If U_off > 0 (cross-offshore), score f(r) from U_off alone, then multiply by an alongshore factor
  for texture and drift:
  - V ≤ 10 km/h: ×1.0;
  - 10-20 km/h: ×0.85;
  - 20-30 km/h: ×0.65;
  - > 30 km/h: ×0.45.
- If U_off ≤ 0, treat it as onshore (below), using the full speed U. Cross-onshore is bad in every
  source.

### Onshore (U_off < 0)
The physics says onshore wind hurts more than offshore helps: earlier spilling, smaller and narrower
overturns, a wider surf zone. Practitioners say onshore wind cuts quality in proportion to speed, and
anything above about 10 kn ruins most beach breaks. Suggested fractions (sustained speed):

| Onshore wind | Fraction |
|---|---|
| < 5 km/h | 0.85 |
| 5-10 | 0.70 |
| 10-15 | 0.50 |
| 15-20 | 0.35 |
| 20-30 | 0.20 |
| > 30 | 0.05 |

As an option, add +0.05 for long-period groundswell (Tp ≥ 12 s) in the 5-15 km/h bands. Practitioners
say short-period swell suffers more under onshore wind, but this bonus is judgement.

### Check against the anchor case
Maroubra 12 Apr 2026: solid groundswell, U_off ≈ 21 km/h, so r ≈ 1.1 and f ≈ 0.91. Gusts give
r_g ≈ 2.6, so no penalty. Wind score ≈ 0.9 of full, against 0.4 now. Spray off the lip is what an
r ≈ 1 offshore over a fast, plunging groundswell looks like.

## Evidence vs judgement

| Element | Status |
|---|---|
| Offshore wind gives later, shallower, more plunging breaking and a narrower surf zone; onshore the reverse | **Evidence:** two independent labs, one field-scale study, theory and simulation |
| Measuring wind relative to wave speed (U/C) | **Evidence:** it is the parameter all the studies use |
| Shape benefit levels off near U/C ≈ 0.4 and doesn't fall up to U/C ≈ 1.2 | **Evidence:** one field study of 22 waves at one site, a pool not an ocean |
| Lab plunging persisted at U/C ≈ ±5 | **Evidence** (secondary description of Douglass and King & Baker) |
| Strong offshore costs paddling, spray, late drops; small waves suffer most | **Practitioner consensus**, not measured |
| "Still good" up to about 15 kn (28 km/h), experienced surfers only above that | **Practitioner rule of thumb** (two guides), no rigorous source |
| Bigger and longer-period swell tolerates stronger offshore | **Evidence for the mechanism** (U/C scaling; opposing-wind attenuation rises with steepness), **judgement for the exact numbers** |
| The breakpoints in f(r) (0.8 / 1.3 / 2.0 / 3.0) and the fractions | **Judgement**, anchored to the field-tested range, the practitioner 15 kn line and the Maroubra case. Calibrate against `backtest.csv` and the lifeguard-reported days. |
| Gust handling via r_g, alongshore factors, onshore table | **Judgement**, consistent in direction with all sources |
| Komar & Gaughan breaker height used for C_b | Standard engineering formula, approximate. Any reasonable H_b estimate works, because C_b only varies with its square root. |

**Gaps:**
- No published study measures surfer-rated quality against offshore wind speed.
- The Global Surf Index and Somo index thresholds sit behind paywalls or blocked pages and remain
  unverified.
- Surfline and Magicseaweed don't publish their wind logic.
- The best next step is local data: score past sessions with known outcomes (the lifeguard reports,
  surf-history.md) under the new rule and tune the 1.3 / 2.0 breakpoints.
